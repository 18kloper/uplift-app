How we decided

Matching forty-eight founders to thirty-seven mentors involves a dozen judgement calls, and most programmes never write them down. These are ours: what we chose, what we chose against, and what each decision costs. Where a decision has a price, the price is named.

Decision one

The whole room is solved at once

The obvious way to match a cohort is one founder at a time: open the list, take whoever is next, give them the best mentor still free. We do not do that, and it is worth explaining why, because the obvious way is quietly unfair.

The problem with going in order

Whoever gets served first spends everybody else’s options

Imagine two founders who both want the same mentor. One of them has ten mentors who would suit them well. The other has exactly one. If the founder with ten happens to be higher up the list, they take the shared mentor, and the founder with one drops to a poor match. Nothing was gained. One founder went from excellent to excellent-by-another-route, and the other went from excellent to barely workable.

That is not a hypothetical. It is what happens by default, and the person it happens to never finds out, because there is nothing to compare against.

So every approved founder and every available mentor go into one problem, solved together. Where two founders want the same person, the one with fewer good alternatives gets them. Which means a founder is sometimes handed a mentor who is a slightly different fit from their own top option, so that the group as a whole is better matched.

What that changed

The highest-scoring mentor is not always the one you get

Every pairing is scored on five things: how well the expected number of sessions lines up, focus areas, the overlap between what a founder said they need and what a mentor offered in their own words, company stage, and whether their availability has a window in common.

But the score decides how good a pairing is, not which pairings get made. A mentor who is marginally better for one founder and the only strong option for two others does not win. When we tested the two approaches against the same applications, going in order left eight founders below a strong match; solving together left one.

The trade

A founder can end up with their second-best mentor rather than their first. What they get in exchange is that nobody in the room is carrying a bad match so that they could have a marginally better one.

Decision two

Every mentor who volunteered gets a founder

Mentors are volunteers. They filled in a form, named their availability, and offered their time to somebody they had never met. Telling one of them there was nobody for them is a real cost, and we decided it is a cost we do not pay for a better-looking set of matches.

The rule

One founder each before anyone gets two, and never more than two

A mentor offering seven to ten sessions could, on paper, carry three founders. We cap it at two. Somebody who volunteered for one deep relationship did not volunteer to run three shallow ones, and the arithmetic working out is not the same as the offer being honoured.

Nobody is booked past the number of sessions they offered, and nobody is given fewer than three. Both fall out of one rule: a mentor carries at most their offered sessions divided by three.

The trade, stated honestly

Optimising purely for match quality and letting mentors sit out would put ten more founders on an excellent-or-better match. It would also mean telling six more volunteers there was no founder for them. We took the mentors’ side of that, on the grounds that a founder on a strong rather than excellent match still has a good mentor, and a mentor sent away has nothing and probably does not come back.

The one exception

A mentor waits rather than a founder taking a bad match

The rule above has a limit. If the only founder left for a mentor would be a genuinely weak fit, we do not force the pairing to keep the mentor busy. That serves the mentor, not the founder. The mentor waits for the next cohort or a later arrival, and the founder goes to somebody they actually suit.

On the current cohort that applies to two mentors, and in both cases for a fixable reason: their applications arrived without focus areas or a stage preference, so nothing in the system can see what they are good at. That is a form problem, not a judgement about them.

Decision three

Three sessions is a promise. More is a preference.

Founders choose how much time they want: the three-session minimum, four to six, or seven to ten. Mentors choose the same. Those two numbers rarely line up perfectly, and how you break the tie says a lot about what you think the programme is for.

The choice

The better-fitting mentor wins, even with fewer hours to give

Every founder gets at least three sessions. That is the promise and it is always kept. Above the minimum, session count is a preference, and it loses to fit.

If the mentor who genuinely understands your business has four sessions to give, and a more available mentor is a weaker fit, we will hand you the better mentor every time. You get more out of four hours with the right person than ten with the wrong one.

We had this weighted wrongly at first, and it showed. Session shortfall was priced high enough that the system would trade a genuinely better mentor for a more available one — the exact opposite of the deal on offer. It is now weighted so it can only ever choose between two mentors of equal quality.

What this means for you

If you asked for seven to ten sessions and your mentor has fewer, that is not an oversight and not a downgrade. It means the alternative was somebody who fit you less well. Your mentor is never booked past what they offered, either — so the answer is never that they overcommitted.

Decision four

The peer rooms are built on stage, not geography

Alongside a mentor, every founder sits in one of five peer cohorts — Edison, Hopper, Bardeen, Lawrence and Morrison. A mentor gives you expertise. A peer room gives you company, which is a different good and fails for different reasons.

How the rooms are cut

Sorted by how far along you are, then adjusted three ways

All founders are lined up by stage — from shaping an idea through to scaling an operation — and cut into five rooms of nine or ten. A peer room only works when the problems in it rhyme: an idea-stage founder sitting with a growth-stage founder gets mentoring, not an exchange between equals.

From there, founders move between neighbouring rooms for three reasons, in this order. First, that everybody in a room can make the same meeting time, because a room with no common window never actually meets and nothing else about it matters then. Second, that each room holds a spread of industries rather than five of one, so the perspectives are fresh and nobody is sitting across from a competitor. Third, that each room contains people who have already done what the others are setting out to do.

Region was considered and left out. The fall sessions are virtual and a third of founders did not state a county, so grouping by geography would have fought stage for nothing. It would only earn a place if these rooms met in person.

Where this one has a weakness

The earliest rooms have nobody who has done it before

Peer experience climbs with stage, and it has to. In a room of idea-stage founders, nobody has raised, hired or sold anything yet. Stage cohesion and peer experience genuinely pull against each other at the early end, and no arrangement of the same people fixes it.

So we say it rather than hide it: the two earliest rooms will get more out of a facilitated conversation, or an alum in the chair, than out of waiting for a peer to have the answer. That is a programme decision, and naming it is better than quietly trading away the thing that makes those rooms coherent.

Decision five

What we are actually short of

Some problems are matching problems. This one is not, and it would be dishonest to present a tidy set of pairings without saying so.

190 offered · 329 asked

The mentor pool is short on hours, not on people

Founders asked for 329 one-to-one sessions between them. The mentors who volunteered offered 190. Twenty founders asked for seven to ten sessions; seven mentors offered that much.

No amount of clever matching closes a gap like that. It means a number of founders land nearer the three-session minimum than the range they picked — not because anything went wrong, but because that is the honest shape of the pool. More mentors with high availability is the only thing that buys more depth.

Every match is confirmed by a person before it is real. The system builds the case and shows its reasoning; it never assigns anybody. And if a pairing is not working, saying so is a normal part of the programme rather than a complaint — rematching is expected, not exceptional.
