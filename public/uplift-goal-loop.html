TechUnited:NJ  ·  Uplift Mentorship Program

A goal, followed all the way through

Every founder in Uplift names a goal before they meet anyone. They sharpen it in week one, work it across three structured meetings, and answer for it at the end. This is what we ask, what it tells us, and what each founder walks away holding.

Stage 01

The application is the baseline

Two forms, one for founders and one for mentors, built on a shared vocabulary. Nothing in the application is asked out of curiosity. Every field either decides eligibility, feeds the match, or establishes a starting point we will measure against later.

What we ask founders

Eligibility and commitment

7 questions  ·  yes or no  ·  gating

  • Have you participated in Uplift before?
  • Do you identify as an aspiring founder, founder, CEO, or small business owner?
  • Are you a resident of New Jersey?
  • Can you commit to a minimum of three one-hour 1:1 mentoring sessions?
  • Can you commit to a minimum of 1.5 hours of educational programming?
  • Will you attend the in-person event on October 27 in Newark?
  • Do you understand that while this program is open to all, we focus on supporting women and minority founders?

It tells us who is actually available for the work. These run before anything else, and a no on the wrong one ends the application there.

It also does something quieter that matters more later. Every attendance expectation we hold a founder to traces back to a yes on this page, in their own hand, before they were accepted. Nothing we ask for at the end is a surprise.

The company snapshot

12 questions  ·  the economic baseline

  • Are you currently raising capital?
  • Are you currently hiring?
  • Do you currently have employees, and how many full-time?
  • Are you currently generating revenue?
  • Are you actively looking for customers or pilot partners?
  • Are you seeking strategic partnerships?
  • Have you previously raised outside capital?
  • What stage is your company currently in?
  • Approximately how much revenue in the last 12 months?
  • Any major milestones you expect to hit during the mentorship period, and what are they?

It gives us the "before" picture for every hard outcome we later claim. A first customer, a first hire, a first dollar of revenue only counts as movement if we recorded that it had not happened yet.

The milestone question does double duty. It captures what the founder already expects to achieve in the same window the program runs, which keeps us honest about what the program caused and what would have happened anyway.

Goal architecture

7 questions  ·  two deliberate funnels

  • Which best describes where you are on your company-building journey?
  • What would make this program successful for you? Select up to two.
  • Which of those is most important to you right now? Select one.
  • Which topics would you most like to explore with your mentor? Select up to three.
  • Of those, which is your primary focus area?
  • How confident do you currently feel navigating your top focus area?
  • Which best describes the current stage of your company?

This is the part the whole program hangs on. By the end of this page a founder has named one focus area, ranked one definition of success above all others, and put a confidence score against the thing they are worst at.

The narrowing is the point. Three topics down to one, two success criteria down to one. A founder who is allowed to want everything arrives with nothing a mentor can act on in an hour.

Motivation, scheduling, and reach

15 questions  ·  match inputs and reporting

  • What about mentorship feels valuable at this stage of your business?
  • What are you hoping to accomplish through your mentorship?
  • What will you bring to the mentorship relationship?
  • What level of availability can you commit to: 3 sessions, 4 to 6, or 7 to 10?
  • Any known scheduling constraints over the program period?
  • Time-of-day preference and preferred meeting method
  • I commit to responding to my mentor within 72 hours
  • What type of mentor would be most helpful right now?
  • Gender, ethnicity, age range, income range, and county (optional, aggregated only)

The third question is a filter, not a formality. Mentorship is a two-way commitment, and founders who cannot articulate what they bring tend to be the ones who go quiet in week four.

Availability is self-selected into a tier rather than assumed, so a founder who commits to seven sessions is held to seven. The 72-hour checkbox becomes the service standard the program team enforces all season.

Demographics are voluntary and reported only in aggregate. They are what let us show reach across New Jersey against the communities the program exists to serve.

What we ask mentors

The mentor application

Mirrors the founder form where it counts

  • Why are you interested in serving as a mentor?
  • What are you hoping to provide through mentoring?
  • What are you hoping to get through mentoring?
  • Mentorship focus areas: select up to three
  • Founder stage preference: select all that apply
  • 1:1 availability and mentoring capacity
  • I commit to responding to my mentee within 72 hours
  • What is your superpower?
  • I understand I will be asked to confirm mentorship engagement at program end

The mentor picks up to three focus areas from the same list the founder picks from. That shared vocabulary is what makes a match a match instead of a guess, and it is why the program can rank every possible pairing rather than assign them by hand.

The give-and-get pair is deliberate. Mentors who name something they want out of it stay engaged longer than mentors who frame it purely as charity.

One consent line does real work at the end of the season: mentors agree at intake that they will be asked to confirm the engagement, so the closing sign-off is an expectation rather than an ask.

What the application leaves us holding, per founder: a verified eligibility record, a company baseline across stage, revenue, headcount and funding, a named primary focus with a confidence score, a ranked definition of success, a stated availability tier, and a voluntary demographic profile. All of it captured before the founder has met a single person.

Stage 02

Week one turns a topic into a target

A focus area is not a goal. "Fundraising" is a category. Week one is where the portal makes the founder say what they actually mean, in their own words, with a date attached.

The portal opens by replaying the founder's own application answers back to them: their primary focus, their secondary areas, what they said they were hoping to accomplish, what they said success looks like. Then it asks them to be specific. Both prompts are required, and nothing further in the program unlocks until they are submitted.

The two required prompts

Week 1  ·  gated  ·  saved permanently

  • Primary focus. What does real progress on this look like for you by November?
  • Secondary focus. What is the one thing that would move the needle here this fall?

The example shown in the field: "I want to close my first 3 paying customers and have a clear pricing model."

This is the difference between a category and a commitment. "Go-to-market" cannot be evaluated in November. Three paying customers and a pricing model can.

The founder is told upfront that these answers will be revisited at the end of the program, which changes how carefully they are written.

Everything typed here is stored and surfaced in a dedicated My Goals and Reflections tab that builds across the season. The founder keeps it.

Stage 03

Discover, Act, Roadmap

Three meetings, each with a job. The arc runs from context to action to a plan that outlasts the program. One hour each, logged in the portal right after they happen, with an AI note-taker running.

Discover

Meeting 1  ·  within 7 days of the match

The job: context and trust. Pick the problem worth your hours together.

Bring
  • Your goals reflection from the portal
  • Where the company actually is: traction, runway, blockers
  • The one decision you keep circling
Leave with
  • One focus area you both agree matters most
  • What winning looks like by November
  • Your next meeting on the calendar

Act

Meeting 2  ·  within 10 days of Discover

The job: turn Discover into a move. Advice becomes action here.

Bring
  • What you tried since the last meeting, including what flopped
  • Real numbers, real customer conversations
  • The obstacle that surprised you
Leave with
  • One concrete commitment with a date on it
  • An experiment, a decision, or an intro your mentor can make
  • A clear picture of what to measure before the next meeting

Roadmap

Meeting 3  ·  by October 23

The job: zoom out. Build the 90-day plan that runs past graduation.

Bring
  • Results from your Act commitment
  • An honest read on what has changed since Discover
  • Your biggest open question for the next quarter
Leave with
  • Your next three moves, in order
  • The signals that tell you it is working
  • An agreement on staying in touch, on your terms

Discover and Roadmap stay the spine. Everything after is another Act.

Three meetings is the floor, not the expectation. Founders self-select into a tier of three, four to six, or seven to ten sessions at application. However many they run, meeting one is always Discover and the closing meeting is always Roadmap. Every session in between is another Act, going deeper on the work between milestones, and each one is logged the same way.

How a meeting gets logged

Submitted in the portal, right after

  • Date of the meeting
  • Estimated session length in minutes
  • Transcript from the AI note-taker, or written key takeaways with a 350-character minimum

Every log is reviewed. AI vetted, human verified. Sessions recorded under 60 minutes count as half credit toward the three-hour threshold, which is what keeps the minute counts honest without policing anyone.

The Roadmap is not the end of the arc. It is what a founder carries into the in-person event on October 27 as their launch pad for the room.

What the closing report adds on top

Alongside the mirrored questions, the exit survey captures what only a founder at the end of the program can tell us. Six statements test the five promises made in week one. A direct question asks whether they discovered New Jersey resources they did not know existed. A count of people they would reach out to again measures the network effect. Match quality is asked of both sides of every pair, using the same question, so a mismatch is visible from two angles rather than one.

The mentor completes a separate short survey at the same time. It confirms whether the engagement was completed, roughly how many hours it took, how productive the sessions were, and how much progress the mentor saw from their own vantage point. That gives every founder's outcome an independent second read rather than resting on self-report.

Both sides then sign a verification form certifying that the logged sessions and educational attendance are accurate. The countersignature is what turns a season of self-reported activity into a documented record, and it is the final key that unlocks the founder's numbered certificate of completion.

Nobody is asked to remember how far they came. We already wrote it down.

Matched against a real Fall 2026 mentor: a founder and business-innovation professor whose application listed go-to-market, pitch and narrative, and hiring and leadership as her focus areas, a 4-to-6-session capacity, and flexible availability. The score builds the shortlist. It does not make the decision. A program manager reviews every pairing before it is announced, which is exactly why a blank field like founder-stage preference is visible here rather than hidden inside a single number.

What she wrote in week one

Her application said her focus was "pitch and narrative." The portal made her say what that actually meant:

"By November I want a deck I'm not embarrassed by, and three investor conversations that go past the first meeting."

That sentence is the difference between a category and something a mentor can act on in an hour, and something we can evaluate in November.

How the three meetings ran

Discover  ·  day 5

Agreed the deck was not the real problem. The story was. Picked the narrative as the focus and put Act on the calendar before leaving.

Act  ·  day 14

Rewrote the opening two minutes together, then made a commitment with a date: pitch it live to three people who are not investors before the next session.

Roadmap  ·  day 38

Two of the three practice pitches had gone badly and the third landed. Built the 90-day plan off what changed, plus an intro the mentor offered to make.

She logged five sessions in total against a four-to-six commitment, 312 minutes. Sessions four and five were both Acts, working the plan between milestones.

A program that only reports the founders who advanced a stage is not measuring anything, it is selecting. The confidence delta, the delivered success criterion, and the two concrete outcomes are what genuinely moved in eight weeks, and they moved on the goal she named herself before she met anyone.

Multiply this by roughly sixty founders a year and it becomes the program report: stage advancement across the cohort, confidence deltas by focus area, success criteria promised against delivered, and a count of the hard outcomes underneath.
